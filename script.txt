Hi, this is my JSC370 final project on global natural disaster patterns using NASA EONET data.

In simple terms, I wanted to answer one question:
What patterns do we see in global disaster observations, and can those patterns help with prediction?

I used NASA’s EONET API and focused on four parts:
category imbalance, time trends, seasonality, and spatial distribution — then I tested prediction performance.

So let me walk you through the website.

On the Home page, I give a short overview and show the key interactive plots.
The first thing that stands out is that the dataset is very imbalanced: wildfires dominate the records.
That matters, because if one category is so large, it can drive the overall trend and hide what’s happening in smaller categories.

Then on the Interactive Visualizations page, I start with category dominance.
You can clearly see wildfire counts are much higher than the others.

Next, I show the time trend.
At first glance, total observations rise a lot in recent years.
But once I break it down by category, it’s clear that this increase is not equally shared — wildfire contributes most of the surge.
So the big message is: the aggregate trend is real in the data, but composition matters.

After that, I look at seasonality.
The monthly heatmaps and line views show that seasonal patterns are different across categories.
Some categories are much more concentrated in specific months than others.
So seasonality exists, but it’s not one universal pattern.

Then I move to the map section.
You can see strong geographic clustering.
But I interpret this carefully: this is a map of observed and reported events, not a perfect map of intrinsic risk.
So clustering can reflect both hazard activity and where monitoring/reporting is more continuous.

Now for modeling.
I compare four models: a seasonal baseline, OLS, GAM, and XGBoost.
I keep the same strict split for all of them: training data through 2023, testing on 2024 and after.
That way the comparison is fair and truly out-of-sample.

The result is interesting: more flexible models don’t automatically give a big overall improvement.
Performance gains are mixed and category-specific, and the dominant wildfire category is still hard to predict well.
So even when results are “not dramatically better,” that’s still useful — it tells us the bottleneck may be feature signal, not just model complexity.

Finally, the Report page has the downloadable PDF with full methods, statistical tests, model comparison tables, and conclusions.

So to wrap up:
This project shows clear global patterns in the EONET data, but also shows why interpretation needs caution.
These data reflect both real hazards and the monitoring system itself.
And from a modeling perspective, adding flexibility helps in some places, but does not fully solve prediction under the current feature set.

Thanks for listening.
